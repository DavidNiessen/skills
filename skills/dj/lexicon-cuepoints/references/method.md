# Automatic draft algorithm

This is a reproducible heuristic for constant-tempo, approximately 4/4 electronic dance music. It is not a learned structural or vocal classifier. The thresholds below are tunable screening choices, not measured confidence probabilities. Listen where possible before treating its labels as musical ground truth.

1. Decode mono at 8 kHz through ffmpeg signed 16-bit PCM. Apply third-order zero-phase Butterworth filters at 35–180 Hz and 250–3000 Hz. Compute centred 25-sample RMS envelopes and retain every 16th sample (2 ms spacing). This reduces filter delay; it does not isolate stems.
2. Enumerate the saved beat lattice `anchor + n * 60 / bpm`, including valid beats before a positive relocated anchor. Average both envelopes within each complete beat. Require at least 64 complete beats.
3. For each adjacent beat compute the Euclidean change between the two log-energy values, with an amplitude floor of `1e-5`. Retain changes at or above the 90th percentile. Sum these by beat index modulo four. The highest total supplies an inferred bar phase. Compare it with the runner-up: a ratio below 1.08 is a review flag, not proof that a ratio above 1.08 is correct. Missing/zero evidence is unresolved.
4. Average four beats into each bar. Define an active bass groove when low-band energy exceeds 38% of its track-wide 80th percentile and high-band energy exceeds 25% of its corresponding percentile. Both percentile energies must exceed .001. Bridge bounded gaps of at most two bars as possible fills. Keep active runs of at least eight bars. These are bass-groove candidates, not verified vocal-free mixing sections.
5. A is the first sustained groove candidate. A return candidate needs at least four consecutive inactive bars immediately before an eight-bar groove. C is the first such candidate. B is a countdown within that preceding break: try 32 bars for trance/house, 16 for other longer tracks, or 8 for tracks shorter than three minutes; then try smaller 16/8 options without crossing time zero. If the break is shorter than eight bars, omit B. Musical review may choose an earlier countdown outside the break when appropriate.
6. For D/F consider later return candidates whose break begins at least eight bars after C and beyond 20% of the analyzed bars. Choose the longest qualifying bass reduction; D marks its start and F its return. E is the longest permitted 32/16/8 countdown that fits within that break. If there is no supported pair, leave D/E/F empty. The selected return need not literally be the song's second chronological drop: F means the main later return in this cue convention.
7. G and H count back 16 and 8 bars from the exclusive end of the last sustained groove. Omit any countdown that does not fit inside that groove. This marks an exit window before the groove ends, not necessarily an outro or the end of audible music. Use the actual exclusive beat boundary, including when the final bar reaches the analyzed endpoint.
8. Emit cue positions 0–7 with the stable slot meanings. Permit equal times across different positions when roles coincide. Validate numeric bounds and supported schema values before generating any mutation plan. Short/weak/segmented inputs are reportable skips; low phase confidence is held for review by default. Do not manufacture positions for skipped tracks.

The script does not analyze harmonic novelty, melodies, speech or singing, and can confuse kick-design changes with breakdowns. Dense Schranz, low-cut intros, fake drops, double-time grids and continuous grooves deserve particular attention. It also cannot establish 8/16/32-bar phrase origins from four-beat phase alone. Review structural boundaries with waveform/spectral detail and listening; use an appropriate validated downbeat/structure model when available. Never advertise this heuristic as listening-verified or universally accurate.

For every track, retain proposed times and review reasons. An exact countdown is relative to the detected event: e.g. at 155 BPM, 16 four-beat bars equal 24.774194 seconds. Decimal serialization preserves the lattice; it does not imply microsecond accuracy in the musical interpretation.
